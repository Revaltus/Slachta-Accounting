*By Slachta Accounting & Consulting Services LLC, a CPA firm serving clients in Minnesota, Wisconsin and nationwide*

Long-term care is one of the most manageable big expenses in retirement, as long as you plan for it early. We work with business owners, dentists, and attorneys across Minnesota and Wisconsin who have built substantial retirement accounts and real estate equity, and most have never run the numbers on what a two-year stay in assisted living would mean for that plan. Treat it as a funding decision, made years ahead with real numbers, and it becomes a choice you control.

## What long-term care actually costs

In Minnesota and Wisconsin, a private nursing home room commonly runs $90,000 to $120,000 a year. Assisted living tends to fall between $50,000 and $65,000, and a home health aide working 40 hours a week can add $50,000 or more. These figures exclude home modifications, medical equipment, and the informal caregiving adult children often absorb.

The average nursing home stay is a little over two years, but many people need care for five years or longer, particularly with dementia-related conditions. That is $180,000 versus $600,000 or more for the same care, which is why long-term care planning is risk management, not budgeting.

## Three ways to fund the risk

Most families combine three sources: long-term care insurance, government benefits (primarily Medicaid and, for veterans, VA Aid and Attendance), and personal savings. Medicare covers very little. It pays for short-term skilled nursing after a hospital stay, generally capped around 100 days, not the custodial care behind most long-term needs.

| Funding source | What it covers | Who it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance | Daily or monthly benefit for home care, assisted living, or nursing home care, usually with a benefit period and elimination period | Clients in their 50s to mid-60s who are still insurable and want to cap their exposure |
| VA Aid and Attendance | Monthly cash benefit added to a veteran's pension for those who need help with daily activities | Wartime veterans and surviving spouses who meet service and income/asset tests |
| Self-funding from savings | Retirement accounts, brokerage assets, home equity, or cash reserves earmarked for care | Clients with $2 million or more in liquid net worth who can absorb the cost without insurance |

## Long-term care insurance vs self-funding

Long-term care insurance makes the most sense for people with significant assets to protect, but not so much that a $600,000 care event is a rounding error. A policy purchased in your late 50s might cost $2,500 to $4,000 a year for a benefit covering $150,000 to $250,000 of care, with inflation riders. You swap a large, uncertain cost for a smaller, known one. We size it the way we size other coverage, as covered in our piece on [how much insurance coverage you actually need](/resources/insuring-where-it-counts-avoid-over-insuring-in-the-wrong-pl-2).

Self-funding fits a smaller group, generally those with $2 million or more in investable assets who can absorb a care event as a large but survivable draw. It works best as a deliberate decision, with assets earmarked and withdrawals coordinated with your financial advisor, since sequencing, tax brackets, and required minimum distributions all interact. The portfolio has to be built for that draw too, as explored in [why a balanced portfolio isn't a set-it-and-forget-it decision](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it).

Most families land in between: a smaller policy that caps the worst case, backed by a savings cushion. There's no single right answer, but waiting and hoping Medicaid or family fills the gap is the one to avoid.

## Veterans benefits many families miss

Wartime veterans and their surviving spouses may qualify for the VA's Aid and Attendance benefit, a monthly payment added to a standard VA pension for those who need help with daily activities like bathing, dressing, or managing medication. It is often several hundred to over a thousand dollars a month, with service, income, and net worth requirements. Full details are on the VA's own [Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefits page](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/).

Families often overlook it, assuming income is too high without checking the thresholds, or not realizing unreimbursed medical and care expenses can reduce countable income. If a veteran or surviving spouse in your family may need care soon, confirm eligibility first.

## Tax treatment of long-term care costs

Long-term care premiums and unreimbursed care expenses can qualify as deductible medical expenses, subject to the 7.5%-of-AGI floor for itemized deductions, with age-based premium limits that adjust annually. [IRS Publication 502 on medical and dental expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) is the reference we use in client tax projections. Business owners should also review whether a C-corporation or certain pass-through structures can deduct premiums for an owner-employee, since the answer depends on entity structure.

## Building long-term care into your broader plan

The clients who handle this well treat long-term care as a line item in retirement planning, like a mortgage payoff date or a succession timeline: a number, reviewed periodically, coordinated across their advisors. For a business owner or dentist in their late 50s, that means three conversations together: what the practice is worth and when it might be sold, what the retirement portfolio can absorb, and whether to buy insurance now while health allows affordable underwriting. We coordinate the tax and cash flow side and loop in your financial advisor for insurance and investments, so the plan actually gets funded.

## Common Questions

**How much does long-term care insurance typically cost?** Premiums vary by age, health, and benefit design, but a policy purchased in your late 50s with a moderate daily benefit and inflation protection commonly runs $2,500 to $4,000 a year. Buying earlier, while you're healthier, generally locks in a lower premium than waiting until your late 60s.

**Does Medicare pay for nursing home care?** Medicare covers only short-term skilled nursing care following a qualifying hospital stay, typically capped around 100 days, and it does not cover the ongoing custodial care that makes up most long-term care needs. That gap is precisely why insurance, VA benefits, or savings need to be lined up separately.

**Can veterans get help paying for assisted living?** Wartime veterans and surviving spouses who meet service, income, and net worth requirements may qualify for the VA's Aid and Attendance benefit, a monthly payment that can be applied toward home care, assisted living, or nursing home costs. Details on eligibility are on the VA's Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefits page.

**Are long-term care insurance premiums tax deductible?** Premiums for a tax-qualified long-term care policy can count as a deductible medical expense, subject to age-based dollar limits and the 7.5%-of-AGI threshold for itemized medical deductions, as outlined in IRS Publication 502.
